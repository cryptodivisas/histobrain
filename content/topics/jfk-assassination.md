## What happened in Dallas?

President John F. Kennedy was fatally wounded on November 22, 1963, while riding in a motorcade through Dealey Plaza in Dallas. Texas Governor John Connally was wounded in the same car and survived. Within hours, Lyndon B. Johnson had taken the oath of office. The events of that weekend can be reconstructed separately from the investigations that later tried to explain them.

## Timeline, November 22–24, 1963

All times are local to Dallas.

- **November 22, about 12:30 p.m.** Gunfire struck the presidential motorcade as it passed through Dealey Plaza. The limousine proceeded to Parkland Memorial Hospital.
- **November 22, 1:00 p.m.** Kennedy was pronounced dead at Parkland.
- **November 22, 2:38 p.m.** Federal judge Sarah T. Hughes administered the presidential oath to Johnson aboard Air Force One at Love Field.
- **November 22, later that day.** Dallas police held Lee Harvey Oswald and charged him with the killings of Kennedy and Dallas police officer J. D. Tippit.
- **November 24.** Jack Ruby shot Oswald during a transfer from Dallas police headquarters. Oswald died before the charges against him could be tried.

The Warren Commission’s opening chapter recounts the weekend, including the transfer of presidential power and the events that prevented a trial. [National Archives, Warren Commission narrative and conclusions](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/warren-commission-report/chapter-1)

## Why the investigations reached different conclusions

**1964, Warren Commission.** The commission concluded that Oswald fired the shots that struck Kennedy and Connally. It reported finding no evidence that Oswald or Ruby was part of a conspiracy to assassinate Kennedy. These were the findings of a federal investigation. Oswald’s death meant there was no criminal verdict on the charges against him. [Warren Commission conclusions](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/warren-commission-report/chapter-1#conclusions)

**1979, House Select Committee on Assassinations.** The committee also found that Oswald fired at Kennedy. It judged a conspiracy probable, relying heavily on an analysis of a Dallas police recording that it interpreted as evidence of a second gunman. It did not identify another gunman or establish the participants in a conspiracy. [House committee summary of findings](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk/select-committee-report/summary.html)

**1982, National Research Council.** A panel reexamined the recording and concluded that the sounds identified as gunfire had been recorded about a minute after the shooting. If those sounds happened later, they could not establish a second shot location at the time of the assassination. This finding addresses the recording that underpinned the House committee’s second-gunman inference. The panel’s remit was the audio evidence. [National Academies, *Report of the Committee on Ballistic Acoustics*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/10264)

## How to evaluate a claim about the records

Start by asking what kind of source you are reading. The time of Johnson’s oath is a chronological fact that can be checked against several records. A commission’s finding is an interpretation of evidence, and it matters which evidence the commission relied on. A newly released document may record a tip, interview, or agency assessment. To evaluate the underlying claim, identify who made it, when they made it, and what independent records support it. The National Archives collection provides reports and records to investigate those questions directly. [National Archives, JFK Assassination Records Collection](https://www.archives.gov/research/jfk)
