## What moment does Botticelli actually show?

Sandro Botticelli painted *The Birth of Venus* around 1485. The goddess already stands on a shell and is arriving at the shore. The winds blow from the left, while a figure to the right holds out a flowered cloak. The title names the myth that makes the picture recognizable, but the depicted scene is the moment of arrival. The painting makes that moment easy to follow by giving the winds, the hair, and the cloak visible jobs. [Uffizi Galleries, The Birth of Venus](https://www.uffizi.it/en/artworks/birth-of-venus)

## Look for the path through the composition

Start at the winds and follow the floating roses and the line of Venus’s hair. Then look at how still the central figure seems compared with the moving things around her. Her pose recalls ancient representations of Venus covering herself, according to the Uffizi. That gives the standing outline a familiar classical form. The waiting cloak extends the story beyond the moment we see: the picture ends while the figure on land is still preparing to receive her.

## Compare the surface with Spring

The Uffizi holds both this work and Botticelli’s *Spring*, often called *Primavera*. *The Birth of Venus* is painted in tempera on canvas, while *Spring* is painted on a wooden panel. They also present different problems for the eye. The first gives Venus a clear silhouette in open space, while *Spring* asks viewers to distinguish many figures against a crowded grove. Compare a section of open sea in one with the vegetation in the other. Each figure becomes readable through a different arrangement of background and outline. [Uffizi Galleries, Spring](https://www.uffizi.it/en/artworks/botticelli-spring)

The Uffizi regards a Medici commission as probable, though it notes that the painting is documented in the family’s villa only later. That is a useful distinction between a documented location and an inference about the original patron.
