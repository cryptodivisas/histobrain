## A portrait of a group getting ready

Rembrandt completed the painting now called *The Night Watch* in 1642 for an Amsterdam civic guard hall. Captain Frans Banninck Cocq stands in dark clothing with a red sash, and his lieutenant Willem van Ruytenburch wears pale yellow. Light makes the two men easy to locate in a dense crowd. The captain’s extended hand draws attention toward the foreground, while figures farther back partly conceal one another. The group is posed as an event in progress, so looking at the painting involves figuring out how individual gestures fit together. [Rijksmuseum collection record](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/The-Night-Watch-Militia-Company-of-District-II-under-the-Command-of-Captain-Frans-Banninck-Cocq--3137deb45cd7765f9a76084a16c99544)

## What happened when the picture moved

In 1715 the painting was moved to Amsterdam’s town hall. Parts of the canvas were cut away to fit its new space, with the largest loss on the left. A smaller painted copy attributed to Gerrit Lundens preserves the earlier layout. The Rijksmuseum used the copy and digital methods to reconstruct the missing areas for a presentation. The reconstructed positions are useful evidence for how the composition originally worked. The recovered areas are a modern interpretation based on an earlier copy, and their painted surface should be understood accordingly. [Rijksmuseum, reconstruction of the missing sections](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/press/press-releases/for-the-first-time-in-300-years-the-night-watch-is-complete-again)

## A two-image looking exercise

Open the surviving painting and the reconstruction side by side. Find the captain in both views, then compare the distances from his body to the left edge. In the reconstructed view, the space and additional figures at the left make the two illuminated officers look less centrally placed. That change affects the impression of movement: a group can look as if it is arriving from somewhere when there is room for the eye to follow it. The comparison is a reminder that the dimensions of an artwork are part of its composition, just as much as its colors and figures.
