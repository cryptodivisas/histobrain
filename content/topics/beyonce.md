## Overview

Beyoncé came to prominence with the Houston vocal group Destiny’s Child. Her 2003 solo debut, *Dangerously in Love*, established a second audience and won the Grammy for Best Contemporary R&B Album. Listening to the group recordings and her solo work side by side reveals a continuity. Even when one voice carries the lead, backing voices can answer a line, repeat part of a phrase, or mark the rhythm.

## How the album became part of the performance

The surprise release of *Beyoncé* in 2013 put songs and their accompanying videos in front of listeners together. *Lemonade* in 2016 used the album-length format to tell a story. Its movement between anger, grief, and reconciliation gives the sequence a narrative function. The accompanying film adds visual settings, poetry, and family imagery. “Formation,” the closing track, takes the story outward, using regional and family references to bring public identity into a record that also deals with private betrayal.

*Renaissance* in 2022 works differently. Its production regularly carries a beat or sound across the point where one track ends and the next begins. The transitions make sense in the context of a DJ set, where keeping people moving is part of the craft. The album draws on house, disco, and other club music traditions, calling attention to the Black and queer communities that helped shape them. The Recording Academy awarded it Best Dance/Electronic Music Album in 2023.

## A listening exercise

Play the end of “Cuff It” into “Energy,” followed by “Break My Soul,” in album order. Listen for which parts of the rhythm persist while the lead vocal and instrumental textures change. Then play “Formation” on its own and again after the preceding tracks on *Lemonade*. The first exercise shows how sequencing can preserve momentum. The second shows how it can change the meaning a listener hears in a song.
