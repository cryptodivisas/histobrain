## Overview

Michelangelo Buonarroti (1475–1564) worked across sculpture, painting, and architecture. His works can feel like self-contained masterpieces in reproductions, but each was made for a particular space and audience. The *Pietà* began as a tomb commission, *David* became a public symbol in Florence, and the Sistine Chapel ceiling turned a room’s curved surfaces into part of the story.

## The Pietà: a sculpture for a tomb

The French cardinal Jean de Bilhères commissioned the [*Pietà*](/topics/pieta-michelangelo) for his burial chapel at the old St. Peter’s Basilica. Mary supports the dead Christ across her lap. Follow the lines of her robe as they spread below his body: the fabric gives the group a broad, stable base and makes room for the weight of an adult figure. The low, compact arrangement suits a work about mourning in a funerary setting. [St. Peter’s Basilica’s account of the commission](https://www.basilicasanpietro.va/en/san-pietro/the-pieta-of-saint-peter-s)

## David: from the cathedral to city hall

Florence’s cathedral authorities commissioned [*David*](/topics/david-michelangelo) in 1501 for a position high on the cathedral. Michelangelo carved the figure from a block that earlier sculptors had already begun working. In 1504, a committee decided to place it outside Palazzo Vecchio, the seat of civic government. The move changed the statue’s public meaning: a biblical figure intended for the cathedral became a symbol of Florence’s independence. David’s fixed gaze and nearly hidden sling suggest that the encounter with Goliath is still ahead. Seen at city hall, that moment of watchfulness could also suggest a republic ready to defend itself. [Galleria dell’Accademia’s collection record](https://www.galleriaaccademiafirenze.it/en/artworks/david-michelangelo/)

## The Sistine Chapel: painting a room

Pope Julius II commissioned the ceiling in 1508. Michelangelo organized nine scenes from Genesis down its center and used painted architectural divisions to help viewers distinguish them. Around that central strip, prophets, sibyls, and other figures occupy different zones. The design rewards two kinds of viewing: from the floor, look for the framework that holds the scenes together, then use a close-up to study details such as the gap between the hands in *The Creation of Adam*.

The altar wall was a separate commission. Michelangelo painted *The Last Judgment* between 1536 and 1541 and organized it around Christ rather than a sequence of rectangular scenes. Comparing the ceiling with the wall shows how the same artist adapted composition to different parts of the same room. ## Try this looking exercise

Open the gallery’s views of *David*. First look at a close-up of the face, then view the entire figure from below. Which details remain legible at a distance? Compare that with the ceiling’s large painted frames. Both works ask the viewer to read a figure or a scene from below, even though one is carved in marble and the other is painted on a vault.
