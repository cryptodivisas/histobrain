## Why does a mob drama start with therapy?

David Chase’s *The Sopranos*, originally broadcast on HBO from 1999 to 2007, follows Tony Soprano, played by James Gandolfini. In the opening episode Tony speaks to psychiatrist Dr. Jennifer Melfi after experiencing panic attacks. He recalls ducks that visited his swimming pool, an ordinary attachment in a story about a man whose job depends on his ability to intimidate others. The conversation gives Tony a place to say things he cannot comfortably tell his family or his criminal associates. It gives the audience a way to test what he says against what the show depicts. ## Watch what changes when the room changes

At home Tony is a parent and husband. With associates he has to assert his position, conceal uncertainty, and judge what others are withholding. In Melfi’s office the pressure changes again: when she asks him to examine an emotion, he can still evade it or shape the story in his favor. Scenes in these settings are more revealing when you hold them together. A genuine feeling for his family can coexist with conduct that hurts them. A sympathetic moment in the office can explain something about a decision without excusing it.

## Try watching the pilot as a conversation

Write down what Tony says he fears, then pick a scene in which he acts on that fear without naming it. Decide which part of your answer comes from dialogue and which part comes from the editing or the performance. That distinction helps explain why the drama can invite sympathy and discomfort at the same time. The series is also useful for understanding the place of subscription cable in American television.
