## Why Mount Fuji looks so small

Katsushika Hokusai designed *Under the Wave off Kanagawa*, popularly called *The Great Wave*, around 1830–32 for the series *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji*. The eye goes first to the folding mass of water, and the boats give it a human scale. Look lower under the crest and there is a quiet triangle: Fuji. The mountain anchors a composition that might otherwise feel as though it were tipping out of the frame. [Metropolitan Museum of Art, impression JP2569](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/39799)

## How a print changes the way you look

Consider the three distances in the design. The white tips of the foam reach into the sky at the front. The rowers bend low in the middle distance. The edges of the mountain meet at a clean point far behind them. A smaller wave in the foreground echoes that triangular outline. Once you see the echo, it becomes harder to look at the main wave without also noticing the mountain. Fuji is the shared subject of the series, but Hokusai lets a different foreground event take the lead in this view.

## A picture designed to exist in multiples

The work is a color woodblock print on paper. The design was prepared for a publishing process in which carved blocks could be inked and pressed onto many sheets. The Met identifies indigo and imported Prussian blue as important to Hokusai’s landscape palette. In a digital reproduction the different blues can seem like a single color until you look at the linework next to the broader areas of water. Compare the two impressions catalogued by the Met as JP2569 and JP10. The layout should be familiar, but differences in inking, wear, paper, and the lighting of the photograph can all affect what you see. That makes it useful to name a museum’s particular impression when discussing a detail. [Metropolitan Museum of Art, second impression](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/36491)
