## A printed book that still looks handwritten

The Gutenberg Bible was produced in Mainz in the mid-1450s. Called the 42-line Bible for the number of lines in most of its columns, it set the Latin Bible in a dense blackletter type. It is a landmark of European printing with movable metal type. That qualification matters: printing and movable type had histories elsewhere before Gutenberg’s workshop. The achievement in Mainz was producing a long, ambitious work in quantity using a European press and type system. ## From separate letters to a complete page

A compositor assembled individual pieces of type into lines. With those lines secured in the press, the workshop could print the same text on many sheets, then reuse the type for another page. A handwritten copy required the scribe to form the letters again each time. The printed columns could nevertheless resemble manuscript pages, because the type was designed to echo familiar lettering. The shared appearance helped the book fit the expectations readers already brought to large religious volumes.

## Why copies still differ

Open the Library of Congress’s digitized Bible and pay attention to the large initials. The text can be reproduced mechanically, while decoration and colored headings can come from work done by hand after printing. Paper and vellum copies also differ in material: vellum is prepared animal skin, and paper was used for many copies. Each surviving book carries a history beyond the press. If two copies have the same line breaks but different initials, you have a clue about which step they shared and which step was individual. That distinction also explains why printing did not instantly eliminate the skills of manuscript production.
